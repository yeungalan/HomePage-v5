# 08 | Studying in Japan #8: Reflections on Thirty Days in Japan

Before I knew it, I had been in Japan for nearly a month. Looking back, it feels different from what I expected: not better or worse, but more real and more three-dimensional.

## The Gap Between Travelling and Living

When I visited Japan as a tourist, what I saw was mostly sights, food and busy shopping districts. Only after actually settling here did I realise that what tourists see is just a small slice of Japan. Most of the lived-in residential areas, the little parks on street corners and the commuter trains full of office workers are places ordinary visitors never go. And it is exactly these places that let me feel what everyday life in Japanese society is really like.

The rhythm of studying has slowed my pace compared with travelling. I have three to four hours of Japanese class a day, and after class I sometimes walk through the nearby residential streets or sit on a park bench to rest and quietly watch the people and things around me. Compared with my old "checklist" style of travel, this slow, immersive rhythm has actually given me a deeper emotional connection to the city.

Tokyo's transport network is incredibly developed, and every major transfer station is a small world of its own. The platforms at JR Tokyo Station are the starting point to every corner of the city: to the immigration bureau in Shinagawa, to the next destination, or just on an aimless trip, it all starts here.

## New Friends from School

Within a month, I made a few new friends from Taiwan and Hong Kong at the language school. Together we went to the Ghibli Museum in Mitaka, Lake Kawaguchi, Meiji Jingu, Harajuku and more. One of them was in Tokyo for the first time, so we explored with him, and the whole thing was full of the surprise of discovering a place for the first time. The joy of sharing someone's "first time" is hard to experience when travelling alone.

In the days ahead, I hope to keep meeting more friends from different places and explore more corners beyond Tokyo together.

## The Reality of Living Alone

Living alone in Japan means handling everything, big and small — cooking, shopping, laundry, paperwork — all by myself. It sounds ordinary, but it takes more energy than I expected. Honestly, not everything went perfectly in the first month: sometimes I ran into resistance because of the language barrier, and sometimes I took detours because I was not familiar with how things work in Japan.

But I think this fumbling and adjusting is a necessary part of growing. There are no shortcuts, only getting familiar and adapting step by step. What I can do is accept the imperfections of the present, and gradually work out my own way of living through each attempt and mistake.

## Hopes for the Future

For me, coming to Japan to study is not only about learning the language, but also a chance to get to know myself again in a completely unfamiliar environment: how I cope when I am on my own, how I solve problems when things are hard, and how I build my own circle in a foreign land.

These first thirty days have been hard, fun and full of meaning. My minimum goal is that by the end of this year I can truly live independently in a foreign country, make like-minded friends, and face all kinds of challenges with a calmer mind.

It is a challenge I am willing to keep taking on.
