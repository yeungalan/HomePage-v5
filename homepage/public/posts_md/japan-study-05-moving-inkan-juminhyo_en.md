# 05 | Studying in Japan #5: Moving Out, Seal Registration and Getting a Residence Certificate

Living in Japan long term, you will inevitably need to move house at some point. Every move requires the "move-out" (転出) and "move-in" (転入) procedures. Seal registration and residence certificates are also documents you will often need in daily life in Japan. Here is the full process for all three.

---

## 1. Moving Out (転出手続き)

To move from your current ward to another one, you first need to file a move-out notice at **your current ward office**.

Taking a move out of Toshima ward as an example:

1. Bring your **residence card** to the Toshima ward office and tell the staff you want to file a move-out (to another ward).
2. The staff give you a move-out form; fill in your details, your current address and the new address you plan to move to.
3. Once done, the staff issue a **move-out certificate** (転出証明書) showing the move-out date, the new address and a QR code.

**Things to watch out for:**
- You must show this move-out certificate when you move in to the new ward, so keep it safe.
- Once the move-out is filed, **all earlier registrations such as seal registration lapse from the move-out date** and can no longer be used, so think carefully about timing.
- The health insurance card issued earlier must also be returned, and you apply for a new one when you move in to the new ward.

---

## 2. Seal Registration (印鑑登録)

Personal seals (hanko) are still widely used in Japan and fall roughly into three types:

- **Mitomein (認印)**: for everyday paperwork such as signing for deliveries; the requirements are low.
- **Ginkōin (銀行印)**: used specifically for banking, needed when opening an account.
- **Jitsuin (実印)**: the most formal type, used for important legal documents such as contracts to buy a car or a home.

International students usually only need a mitomein and a ginkōin, with no need for a jitsuin.

Because I use my English name (Latin letters), my seal is **carved entirely in English**. If your name has a kanji form and you live somewhere that carves Chinese-character seals, having one carved outside Japan and bringing it with you is often cheaper than having it made in Japan.

**Seal registration steps:**
1. Bring your residence card and seal to the ward office counter and say you want to do "印鑑登録" (seal registration).
2. After you fill in the form, the staff check that the name on the seal matches the name on your residence card.
3. Once confirmed, they register it and issue a **seal registration certificate** (印鑑証明書).

The seal registration certificate is your proof whenever the authenticity of your seal needs to be confirmed later; I recommend getting one or two spare copies (fees vary by ward, a few hundred yen each).

---

## 3. Getting a Residence Certificate (住民票の発行)

The residence certificate (住民票) is an official document recording your address and basic details. You may need to submit it for many procedures in Japan, such as opening some bank accounts or applying for a driver's licence.

**Things to note when applying:**

There are several items you can choose to include on a residence certificate, and different institutions **require different items**. If the certificate includes more items than the institution needs, it may even be rejected. So **I strongly recommend checking with the institution (such as a bank) exactly which items it needs before applying**, and applying accordingly, so you do not have to do it again.

**Application steps:**
1. At the ward office, fill in the application form, choose the items you need, and state the purpose (such as "for opening a bank account" or "for a driver's licence").
2. After submitting, pay at the payment counter next to it and wait for your number to be called.
3. When your number is called, collect the certificate.

You can apply for a residence certificate at the same time as your move-in registration, saving a trip back to the ward office.

---

## Summary

Moving out, seal registration and getting a residence certificate are three procedures that come up often in administrative life in Japan. None of them is complicated on its own, but the documents and timing need extra attention, especially the fact that registrations lapse after you move out. I recommend making a list of the documents you need before each visit; it greatly reduces unnecessary back-and-forth.
